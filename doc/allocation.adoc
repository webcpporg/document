[#document-allocators]
== Allocators

A document's storage carries an allocator, as a standard container does, and the document allocates
through it: its image, when it owns one, and the working state of every operation on it. The
default is `std::allocator<std::byte>`, the global heap. A program that gives a
`std::pmr::memory_resource` instead keeps a document's memory where it chooses: in a buffer of its
own, in an arena released at the end of a request, or in a resource that counts. This section names
the storages, shows a document in a resource, lists how documents are made in an allocator, how
moves and copies treat it and what happens when it has no room, names what stays outside it, lists
the reads that allocate in it, and says which of document's types can be shared between
processes.

[#document-allocators-storages]
=== The storages

A document's second template parameter is its storage, `wd::basic_owned<Allocator>` or
`wd::basic_external<Allocator>`, of any allocator of `std::byte`:

[cols="2,2,3",options="header"]
|===
| Storage | Allocator | The image

| `wd::owned`, the default
| `std::allocator<std::byte>`
| the document's own, from the global aligned `operator new`

| `wd::external`
| `std::allocator<std::byte>`
| a buffer its user owns (<<document-images-storage>>)

| `wd::pmr::owned`
| `std::pmr::polymorphic_allocator<std::byte>`
| the document's own, from the allocator's `std::pmr::memory_resource`

| `wd::pmr::external`
| `std::pmr::polymorphic_allocator<std::byte>`
| a buffer its user owns

| `wd::basic_owned<Allocator>`
| any allocator of `std::byte`, a stateful one of the program's own among them
| the document's own, from the allocator

| `wd::basic_external<Allocator>`
| any allocator of `std::byte`
| a buffer its user owns
|===

The namespace `wd::pmr` names the rest for a memory resource, as `std::pmr` does for the standard
containers: `wd::pmr::document`, the default traits' document of `wd::pmr::owned`;
`wd::pmr::basic_document<Traits>`, the same for other traits; its handles `wd::pmr::value` and
`wd::pmr::const_value`; and its parser, `wd::pmr::parser`. A document's `allocator_type` names the
allocator, and `get_allocator()` returns a copy of it.

*An owned image* is allocated, grown and freed through the allocator rebound to blocks of 16 bytes,
16-aligned, `std::allocator_traits<Allocator>::rebind_alloc` of a block, so that the allocator is
asked for the alignment the image's heap needs: a memory resource is asked for an alignment of 16,
which it gives. The allocator must give it. A 16-aligned block is over-aligned wherever
`std::max_align_t`'s alignment is 8, as on arm64 macOS and on MSVC, and an allocator that ignores an
over-aligned request may give less: a buffer that is not 16-aligned is given back at once, and the
operation returns `errc::capacity` and changes nothing, as when the allocator has no room. The
allocator's pointer to the image is kept as the allocator gave it, and the image is given back
through it: an allocator whose pointer is not a plain one, which may carry a segment or a tag
besides an address, gets back what it handed out. Inside the image, every pointer is an offset
(<<document-allocators-shareable>>). The image grows by doubling, as <<document-images-reserve>>
says, a new buffer allocated before the old one is freed. A monotonic resource never reuses what was
freed, so an image built large in one is sized once with `reserve`. The standard allocator's image
comes from the global aligned `operator new` in its form that returns null.

*The working state* of every operation on a document, owned or external, is in the same allocator,
rebound: the records a change stages before it changes anything, the stack of a deep copy and of a
compaction, the parser's frames, path and pending text, its selectors and their callbacks, the
frames of `stringify_to`'s stack past the 16 it has room for and the piece it holds back for its
sink once it holds more than its own room of 4096 bytes, the key a handle of a missing member
holds, the buffers in which a value is prepared to be stored, and the keys `Object.assign` takes
from its source. For an external document that is all the allocator holds. A lookup by key
allocates nothing, whatever the key's length, and neither does a comparison with `==`, which walks
both trees through their nodes' links, nor an erasure, `delete` of a member, `pop_back` or a
truncation, which lists the nodes it erases through the erased nodes themselves and so cannot fail.

[#document-allocators-making]
=== Making documents in an allocator

Each way to make a document has a form that takes its allocator:

[cols="2,3",options="header"]
|===
| Call | The document

| `wd::pmr::document::create(alloc, max_bytes)`, the static `create` of any owned document's type
| a new owned document, holding `null`

| `wd::parse(text, options, alloc)`
| the document of a JSON text (<<document-parsing>>)

| `wd::parse_into(buffer, text, options, alloc)`
| the same in a buffer of the program's, the allocator holding the working state

| `wd::basic_parser<Traits, Storage>(options, alloc)`, or `(buffer, options, alloc)` for an external
  document
| the document a parser builds, the parser's own working state in the same allocator
  (<<document-parsing-chunks>>)

| `wd::from_image(bytes, alloc)`
| an owned copy of an image (<<document-images-bytes>>)

| `wd::format(buffer, alloc)`, `wd::open(buffer, alloc)`
| an external document, formatted or opened in place

| `doc.clone()`, `doc.clone(alloc)`
| a copy, owned, in the document's own allocator or in the one given

| `wd::pmr::document::nothing(alloc)`, the static `nothing` of any document's type
| a document that holds nothing, as a moved-from one does: no image, nothing allocated and no
  failure, its root reading `undefined`, a change and `clone` refused with `errc::type`, until a
  move assignment gives it an image

| `doc.compact_into(buffer)`
| the compact image in a buffer, an external document in the document's allocator
  (<<document-images-compact>>)

| `pop`, `shift`, `splice`, `slice`, `concat`
| the values they return, owned documents in the source's allocator, an external source's
  included (<<document-arrays>>)
|===

[#document-allocators-moves]
=== Moves and copies

A document is allocator-aware as the standard containers are:

* *A move* takes the image and the allocator. The document moved from holds nothing.
* *A move assignment* takes the image when the allocator propagates on a move assignment, or when
  two of its allocators are always equal, as the standard allocator's are. Polymorphic allocators
  of different resources do neither: an owned image is then copied into the target's resource, one
  copy of its bytes, the source's image is freed in its own resource, and the source holds nothing
  after it. An external image, which no allocator holds, is taken as it is. The copy can fail, and
  an assignment has no result to return the failure in: it throws `std::bad_alloc` through
  `boost::throw_exception`, and leaves both documents as they were; without exceptions,
  `boost::throw_exception` is the program's own, which does not return. For allocators that
  propagate or are always equal, the move assignment is `noexcept`.
* *The copy constructor,* with exceptions only (<<document-exceptions>>), asks the allocator's
  `select_on_container_copy_construction`, which for a polymorphic allocator gives the default
  resource, `std::pmr::get_default_resource()`, and not the source's. The allocator-extended copy,
  `wd::pmr::document copy(doc, alloc);`, takes the allocator given, and
  `wd::pmr::document empty(alloc);` makes a document holding `null` in it.

[#document-allocators-failure]
=== When an allocator has no room

*With exceptions,* an allocator that has no room throws `std::bad_alloc`, a type derived from it,
or Boost.Interprocess's `boost::interprocess::bad_alloc`, which its allocators throw and which
derives from `std::exception` alone. The document catches these where the operation can still
return: the operation returns `errc::capacity` and changes nothing, as for a full image. An
allocator that throws anything else ends the program, since what it throws leaves a `noexcept`
operation. A change allocates all of its working state before its first change
to the document, which then changes by steps that cannot allocate, so a failure at any of its
allocations leaves the document as it was. A few places have their own way to say it:

* a parser that runs out fails with `errc::capacity` and stays failed until `reset()`, as after
  any error, and a selector it has no room for is not added: `on` and `observe` return
  `errc::capacity`;
* `stringify_to` stops with `errc::capacity`, its sink not called again, so the sink holds a part
  of the text;
* a handle of a missing member whose key finds no room reads `undefined`, and a change through it
  is `errc::capacity`;
* the move assignment between allocators that differ, which has no result to return the failure
  in, throws `std::bad_alloc` (<<document-allocators-moves>>); and the sugar throws its
  `wd::capacity_error` (<<document-exceptions>>).

*Without exceptions,* an allocator cannot return null, so an allocator that fails ends the program,
as it does for any standard container. A program built without exceptions sizes its resource for
what it does, as the example's 64 KiB are sized for its documents, or gives the resource an upstream
that does not fail. The standard allocator's image is the exception: the `operator new` it comes
from returns null, so an image the global heap has no room for is `errc::capacity` in either build.

[#document-allocators-outside]
=== What stays outside the allocator

A few allocations are not in the document's allocator, each for a reason of its own:

* *A parse in chunks.* When a chunk ends inside a value, Boost.JSON's parser saves where it stopped
  on a stack of its own, so that the next chunk resumes there, and allocates that stack in the
  global heap: Boost.JSON offers no way to place it. It allocates once at the first suspension,
  and again at each suspension deeper than every earlier one, and keeps the last stack until the
  parser is destroyed, across `reset()`. A complete text, given to `wd::parse`, to `wd::parse_into`
  or to the `finish(last)` of a parser that was given no text before, never suspends, and
  allocates nothing there.
* *What the program gets back.* A value returned to the program is of the program's own type, in
  the standard allocator: the `std::string` that `stringify` returns, with the stack it writes it
  with, so that `stringify` asks the document's allocator for nothing, the `std::string` of
  `get<std::string>()` and the other reads into a type that owns its memory, the text of
  `path_view::to_string()`, the message of an error code, and the exceptions of the sugar, which
  hold that message. `get` is `noexcept`, so the standard allocator's failure in a read ends the
  program. `stringify_to` writes to a sink, and so into a string of the program's choosing, as the
  example's `std::pmr::string` is.
* *Literals.* `wd::object`, `wd::array` and `wd::literal` hold their values in the standard
  allocator, as the program builds them. Storing one copies it into the document, in the document's
  allocator.
* *Streams and pools.* The streams of documents, `wd::basic_stream_parser` and
  `wd::async_read_document` (<<document-streams>>), and the shared and leased documents,
  `wd::shared_document` and `wd::leased_document` (<<document-sharing>>, <<document-wire>>), keep
  the default storage: their documents are `wd::owned` or `wd::external`. A `wd::image_pool`
  allocates its blocks once, from the global heap; a `wd::shared_pool` formats memory the program
  gives.

[#document-allocators-reads]
=== Reads from several threads

A standard container allocates nothing in a `const` member, so several threads may read one at
once, whatever its resource. Most of a document's `const` operations allocate nothing either: a
lookup by key, `get` into a scalar or a view, `==`, iteration and the containers' views, `image`,
`compact_size`, and `stringify`, which keeps its text and its stack in the standard allocator. A
few allocate in the document's allocator, rebound:

* `stringify_to`, for a value nested more than 16 containers deep, the frames of its stack past its
  own room, and for a chunk larger than 4096 bytes, the piece it holds back once it holds more than
  its own room;
* `operator[]` of a document or a handle, for a missing member whose key is longer than a
  `std::basic_string` holds without allocating, which its standard library decides: the handle
  holds a copy of the key, and so does each copy of such a handle;
* `clone()`, `slice` and `concat`, which make documents in it, and `compact_into`, whose external
  document keeps it, with their working state.

Several threads that use these on one document at once need a resource that is safe to use from
several threads, as `std::pmr::synchronized_pool_resource` is and as
`std::pmr::monotonic_buffer_resource` and `std::pmr::unsynchronized_pool_resource` are not. The
standard allocator is, so a `wd::document` and the documents a `wd::shared_document` shares are read
by any number of threads at once with every `const` operation (<<document-sharing>>), and so are the
reads into a type of the program's own, `get<std::string>()` among them, which allocate there.

[#document-allocators-shareable]
=== What can be shared

A shareable type is one that can be built in memory that several processes map, each at an address
of its own, and used there in place. document's is the image. Every pointer inside it is an offset
from where it is stored, and every size a 64-bit integer, so it is relocatable and the same bytes at
any address and on every target (<<document-images>>): a `wd::shared_pool` in a segment of shared
memory, the blocks of a `wd::image_pool`, `wd::open` over bytes another process wrote,
`wd::from_image` of them, and external storage over a segment of shared memory all use it as it is.

A `basic_document` object is not shareable, nor are its handles, its parsers, its writers or the
views of the pools: each holds addresses of the process that made it, the image's and its
allocator's among them. Each process that maps an image opens it with a document of its own
(<<document-sharing-processes>>).

[#document-allocators-proof]
=== How it is proven

The tests `allocator`, `allocator_tree`, `allocator_values`, `allocator_parse` and
`allocator_stringify` replace the global `operator new` and `operator delete`, every form, and count
their calls. Each runs the operations it lists on documents in a counting resource over a monotonic
buffer whose upstream is the null resource, and checks that they made no call of the global
`operator new` and that the resource is given back all it handed out: the image made, grown, copied
and moved; the tree's changes and compaction; the handles, the conversions, `Object.assign`, the
array methods and the containers' views; a parse of a whole text, with selectors and revivers; and
`stringify_to` into a `std::pmr::string` of the same resource, at every indent and chunk, a
document nested 10,000 levels deep among them. Where the global heap is reached on purpose, the
count is exact: a parse in chunks calls `operator new` as often as Boost.JSON's stack grows, and
`stringify` as often as its `std::string` and the frames of its stack past its own room need, while
it asks the document's resource for nothing. A dozen small texts written by `stringify_to` in a
64 KiB arena, as the example's, take nothing of it. With exceptions, each operation runs again over
a resource that fails at its first allocation, then its second, and so on until the operation
succeeds, and every failure must be `errc::capacity`, or the `std::bad_alloc` of the places that
throw it, with the document equal to what it was. Both traits are covered, `js_traits` with its
UTF-16 strings and keys among them, with strings and keys longer than any small-string buffer. The
tests run on every target, natively, on emscripten, and for wasm32-wasip2 and wasm32-wasip3
(<<document-testing>>).
